# Fallen Angels intake, October 30, 2026

Root-down: Speculative fiction > Science fiction > Modern-day SF > Humorous science fiction > Satirical SF. One standalone novel, first published in 1991, by Larry Niven, Jerry Pournelle and Michael Flynn. The current Baen product's 2002 date is a reedition, not first publication.

Whole plot: isolated orbital habitats need atmospheric nitrogen. A scoopship is shot down, leaving two astronauts stranded on a cold, anti-technology Earth. Science-fiction fans rescue them, evade the authorities and smuggle them across the country. A museum Titan cannot provide escape; the fans instead recover and fuel the Phoenix spaceplane. Chapter 20 rejects a violent last stand and completes the launch. Chapter 21 shows the spaceplane landing in Grenada and plans another orbital trip. The government is not overthrown.

Satirical SF is the best fit. The publisher's legal acknowledgment explicitly calls the society satirical. Independent whole-book reviews describe the humorous plot, political caricature, fandom jokes and fan-convention culture. Institutions, ideological certainty and fannish self-myth are recurring targets across the rescue journey, not incidental quips. Comic adventure SF is a real rival, but institutional ridicule is more distinctive than escapade chaos. Rom-com SF fails because courtship does not organize the comedy. The existing Humorous SF mode exception admits future-set books despite its Modern-day parent; no definition is being changed.

Orbital mechanics (6 fictions) is the strongest subject rival. Scoop velocities, freefall adaptation, microwave warming, scarce volatiles, liquid oxygen/hydrogen, inertial guidance and rendezvous constraints materially shape the rescue. These are substantial, not dismissed to manage a shelf count. Still, the whole novel is a satirical fan rescue and road adventure rather than sustained physics-first problem solving. Author reputation alone does not establish Hard SF. Its disputed climate premise is fiction, not endorsed climate science.

Dystopian rebellion (9) is another genuine rival: an established coercive state, police pursuit and an underground resistance. It loses because the central goal is rescue, escape and renewed space access, not bringing down the regime. The ending explicitly avoids a last stand. Orwellian propaganda, state control, class control and assimilation resemble parts of the coercive society but do not outweigh the sustained comic rescue. Huxleyan control fails because manufactured pleasure and willing consent are not the engine.

Climate tyranny (1), Climate breakdown (2), Displacement (3) and Climate class divide (5) each catch real features: criminalized energy use, advancing ice, forced journeys and separated orbital survivors. None describes the whole novel as closely. The plot is not primarily necessary rationing, climate migration, enclave inequality or rebuilding after catastrophe. Ruined Earth and post-holocaust routes similarly overvalue the backdrop. The principal arena is an Earth road journey, not colony civic life, interstellar adventure or space war. Comic space fiction (9) loses on that arena, not to avoid a tenth card. Planetary science, matter transmission and conceptual hard SF lack the defining engine.

Historical SF and alternate history fail: this was a near future at publication, not a deliberate closed-period setting or a specified historical divergence. Its old communication technology does not retroactively make it historical SF. Time travel, parallel worlds and fabulation fail: references to real fans and SF books are not literal world crossing or authored-reality mechanics. Fantasy fails because there is no demonstrated magic; the title's angels are astronauts.

Corpus: the current canonical ISFDB record 988116 lists no series. SFE discusses Fallen Angels separately from Dream Park and The Descent of Anansi. Nicoll disputed an older ISFDB grouping; that catalog complaint is not evidence of a shared universe. No other novels are merged. Research used legal chapter 1 and chapters 19-21 plus independent whole-plot reviews, not a claimed complete front-to-back reading. Moderate-high confidence. Ordinary placement, Satirical SF 5 to 6. No seed, split, definition change or existing-card move.

## Contradictions and limits
Current Baen metadata gives the 2002 edition, while ISFDB records the original July 1991 publication and contemporary 1991 reviews. Only the year is used. The Libertarian Futurist Society's appreciation calls it a 1992 novel, confusing the award year; it is not used for publication dating. Reviews disagree about the exact crash location; no precise location is asserted. Positive reviewers endorse the science, while Nicoll disputes the climate premise. This record does not endorse the fictional climate model. No unauthorized full-book host was used. Primary reading was the first and last three chapters, supplemented by whole-book reviews, not the entire novel. Old ISFDB grouping is disputed and absent from the current canonical record, so no shared-universe merge is justified.

## Result
Satirical SF: 5 to 6 fictions. One fiction and one novel added. Total 805 nodes, 2,622 fictions, 6,032 books. No existing card, definition or structure changed.
