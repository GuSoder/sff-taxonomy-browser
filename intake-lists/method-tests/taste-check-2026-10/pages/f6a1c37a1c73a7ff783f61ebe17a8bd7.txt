# The Year in Review 2025 by Ian Mond 

 February 4, 2026 January 28, 2026 Ian Mond 0 Comments 

![Headshot of Ian Mond](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/locus-3-243x300.png)

Ian Mond (2026)

The Year in Review 2025

2025 was the year of long novels. Not Clarissa by Samuel Richardson long, but not far off. Marguerite Young’s Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965) clocks in at just under 700,000 words, took Young more than 20 years to write, and took me more than six months to read. It’s a reading experience like nothing I’ve encountered – hallucinatory, hyp­notic, and discombobulating. The longest-ever novel about a woman on a bus searching for her childhood nursemaid, the titular Miss MacIntosh.

![Cover of Tom's Crossing by Mark Z. Danielewski](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Danielewski-Toms-Crossing.jpg) But that’s a six-decade-old novel. What about 2025? Well, the second-longest book I read, com­ing in at just under 530,000 words, was Mark Z. Danielewski’s Tom’s Crossing, his first completed work in almost 20 years. It took more than a month to read, but I never regretted a day. The novel is a propulsive, big-hearted Western about a quiet outsider, Kalin March, who, on the promise he makes to his dying friend Tom Gatestone, leads two horses marked for slaughter – Mouse and Navidad – on a perilous quest into the craggy high moun­tains of Utah. Kalin is joined by Tom’s sister, Landry, who refuses to turn back, and by a host of ghosts, including Tom himself. On their heels is the villainous Old Porch and his posse of sons, out to get Kalin not for the horses (which the Porches own), but for the death of Old Porch’s youngest, Russel. It’s hard to capture, in a space this small, everything Danielewski achieves, both structurally and narratively, but Tom’s Crossing is precisely the kind of big novel it’s a pleasure to get lost in.

In comparison to Tom’s Crossing, both Joe Hill’s King Sorrow and Alex Pheby’s Waterblack read like novellas, even if both pass the 200,000-word mark. The former was, by far, the year’s most entertaining novel. A close-knit group of college friends make a pact with an ancient dragon, King Sorrow, after Arthur Oakes is blackmailed into stealing rare books. The dragon deals with the problem violently, but once awakened it demands a victim every year, chosen by the group – refuse, and it will come for you. Unsurprisingly, King Sorrow has already been optioned for television; any creator worth their cinematic salt would be licking their lips at several epic setpieces (the extended passage on a British Airways plane is a humdinger). Couple those dramatic moments with terrific character work, moral conundrums, and mythic undercurrents, and you’ve got a genu­ine page-turner.

Waterblack is also a page-turner, but of a dif­ferent variety. With the final book in the Cities of the Weft trilogy, Pheby continues to undercut the typical narrative arc of epic fantasy. His act of subversion this time is to centre the first chunk of the story on a peripheral character from the previous books. Pheby uses the assassin Sharli to give us a refreshed perspective on the trilogy as a whole, on the events that have transpired, and the effects they’ve had on more than just the main players, like Nathan, his parents, and the Master of Mordew. It all comes together in an extraordi­narily imaginative climax that hints at future stories set in this magnificent world.

It’s not all about long novels, though. In fact, my two favou­rite books of the year, Nina Allan’s A Granite Silence and E.J. Swift’s When There Are Wolves Again, are less than one hundred thousand words.

A Granite Silence is the culmination of the themes and concepts Nina Allan has been toying with for several years. In the style of the great Gordon Burn, who took a novelistic approach to true crime and, in the case of Alma Cogan, imagined an alternative life for the titular British singer, Allan revisits the tragic 1934 murder of eight-year-old Helen Priestly in Aber­deen. In a bid to explore Helen’s death from differ­ent narrative angles, Allan smudges the boundary between fiction, non-fiction, autofiction, and speculative fiction. It sounds overly postmodern, but is in fact a masterclass in voice, as Allan slips seamlessly between different modes of storytelling.

Swift’s When There Are Wolves Again is a thematic sequel to her previous novel, The Coral Bones. Both explore our response – or lack thereof – to climate change, with When There Are Wolves Again being the more hopeful of the two. Told over five decades and toggling between a documentarian and a climate activist, Swift envisages a future in which severe heat and flooding have fundamen­tally reshaped the British landscape, yet where efforts to rewild the land and reintroduce extinct species (like the wolves of the title) offer a modicum of optimism. It’s a smart, beautifully written, and moving novel that presents an au­thentic, rather than doom-laden or rose-coloured, view of the Anthropocene’s impact on our planet, our politics, and our relationships.

Also vying for best book of the year was Em­ily Hunt Kivel’s debut, Dwelling. This timely, absurdist novel sees all the renters in New York evicted from their homes as the city morphs into one vast Airbnb. With few options available, Evie Cavallo heads to her cousin, a real estate agent, in the Texas town of Gulluck. From that point on, surrealism ensues, as Evie, who now lives in a giant cement cowboy boot (the literal woman who lives in a shoe), encounters immortal cobblers, magic keys, and an ancient giant fish. All this wonder­ful strangeness is in service of making a pointed argument about affordability, the housing crisis, and the way society prioritises wealth and power over basic needs.

![Blue cover of Vanishing World by Sayaka Murata](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Murata-VanishingWorld.jpg) It was an honour to be part of Locus’s Recom­mended Reading List for translated fiction this year. I read some superb novels, including Max Besora’s chaotic The Fake Muse, translated by Mara Faye Lethem; Cheon Seon-ran’s unconven­tional but striking vampire novel, The Midnight Shift, translated by Gene Png; Bora Chung’s frenetic space opera, Red Sword, translated by Anton Hur; and Sayaka Murata’s alternative Ja­pan where sex is discouraged, Vanishing World, translated by Ginny Tapley Takemori. Of that strong group, though, my two favourites were Olga Ravn’s The Wax Child, translated by Martin Aitken, and Bora Chung’s second book of the year (also translated by Anton Hur), Midnight Timetable.

Ravn’s short novel is a fictional account of a historical event: the 17th-century witch trials of Danish noblewoman Christenze Kruckow and four of her friends. The Wax Child’s singular narrator is the titular child, shaped in beeswax, who provides an inti­mate perspective on what transpired—the accusations, torture, and betrayal, alongside love, intimacy, gossip, remedies, and spells. Translator Martin Aitken does an excep­tional job conveying Ravn’s poetic voice, portraying a community of courageous women unified by their love for one another.

Bora Chung’s Midnight Timetable – subtitled A Novel in Ghost Stories – is a nightmarish, eerie se­quence of tales centred on a mysterious institution in South Korea, where each locked room holds an object possessed by a ghost. Across seven stories, we encounter haunted handkerchiefs, unsettling phone calls about funeral arrangements, cursed artefacts, and animals (specifically, ghostly sheep) seeking retribution on their murderers. These stories are exceedingly creepy – yes, even the one about the sheep – and showcase Chung’s deep and abiding love for all things ghostly.

Over on Substack, where I discuss the books I don’t review for Locus, I encountered the provoca­tive work of John Pistelli. His fifth novel, Major Arcana, originally serialised on the platform, was released this year by Belt Publishing. It’s a heady blend of comic books (with one of its central figures, Simon Magnus, a hybrid of Alan Moore and Grant Morrison), academia, and tarot cards, topped off with a sprinkle of magic realism. The storytelling is digressive (in all the good ways), challenging (it opens with the shocking suicide of college student Jacob Morrow), and sometimes uncomfortable (especially on questions of gen­der). Yet the novel is never anything other than thoughtful and compassionate, recognising both the power of imagination and creativity and the complexities of being alive in the modern age. I loved it.

In a bid to feel vaguely relevant when award season comes around, I read more novels from the core of the genre. This included Adrian Tchaikovsky’s Shroud, a book that is everything “sense of wonder” science fiction should be. We’re presented with a first-contact narrative set in a future driven by galactic expansion and greed, where humanity, hungry for resources, encounters a planet – Shroud – that emits multiple cacophonous radio signals. Permanently cloaked in darkness, the planet is totally inimical to hu­man life, but, crucially, not to life in general. For plot reasons I won’t go into, the novel becomes an astonishing road trip across a world that is as stun­ning and mysterious as it is frightening and alien.

Speaking of core genre, Emily Tesh followed up her Hugo-winning Some Desperate Glory with the magnificent The Incandescent. It’s a magic school novel that, instead of focusing on the talented kids, centres on a single teacher, Dr. Sap­phire “Saffy” Walden, Director of Magic at the run down, underfunded Chetwood Academy. Tesh covers a single academic year in a world where magic is a fact of life, and where class status, rather than secrecy, determines who gets to wield magic.

Nnedi Okorafor’s Death of the Author is less an example of core genre than a commentary on the genre industry – publishing, fandom, and movie adaptations. The novel follows Zelu, a paraplegic Nigerian American writer who, after being fired from her university job and having her literary novel rejected, smashes out a science fiction novel, Rusted Robots, that becomes a worldwide success (the narrative is punctuated with excerpts from Rusted Robots). Although Zelu’s career tracks a familiar arc – success, anger over the movie adaptation, cancellation, re­demption! – it’s her colourful, confrontational, and loving relationship with her family, both in America and Nigeria, that underpins the novel’s emotional backbone.

If Isaac Fellman sits outside the genre’s core, that may change with Notes from a Regicide, a novel that de­serves award recognition (I’m looking at you, Nebula and Hugo Awards). It is a remark­able, elegiac tale set in the far future that explores revolu­tion, the trans experience, and found families. Trans man Griffon Keming recounts the history of his adoptive parents, Etoine and Zaffre (also trans), who decades earlier fled a failed revolution in the dystopian city of Stephensport. As much as I loved the deliberate lack of explicit worldbuilding, which asks the reader to connect the dots, it was the gorgeous prose – including Etoine’s prison journals – that kept me hooked. Please. People. Read this book.

Some of my favourite authors, including Nick Mamatas, C.D. Rose, Claire North, Angela Slat­ter, Ben Peek, Helen Marshall, James Bradley, and Grady Hendrix, published new novels.

Let’s start with Nick Mamatas. Kalivas! Or, An­other Tempest is exactly what it says on the tin: a reimagining of the Bard’s The Tempest. Mamatas’s take is set at the end of the world – a posthuman future where what’s left of humanity is riddled with nanotechnology that cures all ills, including death. Kalivas, the child of the brilliant (but now deceased) scientist Soteria Kalivas, serves as our Caliban, while the Master of Saint Francis and his daughter Mira, exiled to the Farallon Islands, stand in for Prospero and Miranda. As for Ariel – you’ll have to find out for yourself how Mamatas reimagines that particular sprite. The genius of the novel lies partly in its subversiveness, embrac­ing both the playfulness and the theatricality of the play, and partly in how Mamatas roots the character of Kalivas in his own childhood and Greek identity.

If you read my 2024 Year in Review, you know my favourite book of that year was C.D. Rose’s Walter Benjamin Stares at the Sea. It wasn’t until recently, though, that I discovered he had pub­lished a book in 2025: We Live Here Now. Winner of the Goldsmiths Prize, the novel is a sometimes perplexing, often uncanny, yet always playful cycle of 14 stories bound together by the enigmatic artist Sigismunda Conrad and her controversial 2015 installation, We Live Here Now. From the first story – a profile of Conrad’s work – it’s clear we’re entering a surreal space that, on the one hand, satirises the business of art – the commissions, the tastemakers, the critics, the panderers – but also tips into a terrifying, liminal world where what’s real and what’s not is up for debate.

With Slow Gods, Claire North presents us with a wild, ambitious, layered space opera. Mawukana na-Vdnaze is a resident of the Shine, a hypercapitalist regime. Forced to become a pilot, he dies when his ship enters “the dark,” a hyperspace of sorts, home to something (or some things) incredibly awful. Now reborn and not en­tirely human, Maw escapes the Shine, only to be called into action as a pilot when a supernova threatens multiple worlds. An in­sightful and profound meditation on mercy.

![Cover of The Crimson Road by A.G. Slatter](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Slatter-CrimsonRoad.jpg) The Crimson Road is the latest mar­vellous instalment in Angela Slatter’s Sourdough Universe. In the port city of St. Sinwin, we encounter Violet Zennor on the day her abusive father dies. For a decade, he sought to mould Violet into a weapon, training her to journey into the Darklands to kill her abducted brother and prevent the rise of the powerful Leech Lords. Violet believes she can now walk away, but fate – and the Leech Lords – have other plans. While standalone, this Sourdough novel is deeply enriched by Slatter’s wider mythology. This shouldn’t put you off, but rather encourage you to read all the stories in the Sourdough-verse. You’re going to love it.

Somewhat similar in voice and style to Slatter – namely, prose that sings – is Helen Mar­shall’s The Lady, the Tiger and the Girl Who Loved Death. We start with Sara Sidorova, 18 and pregnant, shot while searching for her husband’s remains. In the heartbeat between life and death, she is transported to a strange realm where the Tiger, Amba, and the Evening Star show Sara her future: the life of her granddaughter, Irenda, who joins the circus and faces off against an authoritarian regime. Elegantly told, The Lady, the Tiger and the Girl Who Loved Death is a story about stories—how myth and folklore are used to shape societies, manipulate nations, and fuel endless wars.

As a massive Grady Hendrix fan, I was very excited to pick up Witchcraft for Wayward Girls, his spin on witches. There’s been a political edge to his previous work (We Sold Our Souls being a good example), but this novel, set in the 1970s and focused on teenage pregnancy and the girls sent away to give birth in secret, is a full-frontal casti­gation of the Republican Party and the Supreme Court’s attack on Roe v. Wade. Within that context, witchcraft (though dangerous) gives Fern and her friends – who have lost their agency, including control of their bodies – a brief taste of power and autonomy. It’s an emotionally devastating novel, and, in my view, Hendrix’s best work to date.

Ben Peek’s The Red Labyrinth might be novella-length, but it has the thematic richness and world-building of a much longer work. Zoja Rose is a wig merchant living an unassuming life in a kingdom ruled by the Red King. But when her lover, Ozias Máutr, is arrested, she becomes a person of inter­est, investigated and apprehended because she can – horror of horrors – read. After nine months of torture, she is led to the red labyrinth, a vast underground city where the living serve the dead. The novella might be high fantasy, but its incisive commentary on power, sacrifice, and oppression resonates with the current moment.

Also resonating, in much the same register as E.J. Swift’s When There Are Wolves Again, is James Bradley’s Landfall. The novel is set in a future Sydney already scarred by debilitating cyclones, rising temperatures, and permanent flooding caused by the Melt. As an off-the-charts cyclone, Nasreen, bears down on the city, Senior Detective Sadiya Azad and Detective Senior Constable Paul Findlay investigate the abduction of five-year-old Casey Mitchell, taken from the Floodline, home to the poor and dispossessed. As a pulse-pounding thriller, the novel ticks all the boxes. But it’s as a commentary on displacement – the impoverished forced out of their houses, the climate refugee forced from their countries – where Landfall truly shines.

I only read a handful of collections in 2025. I came upon Kristina Ten’s Tell Me Yours, I’ll Tell You Mine very late in the year – so late that my review features in this month’s edition. I’m not going to repeat what I say there about this unnerv­ing debut collection, other than to note that Ten is now on my radar: I’ll read whatever she writes. On the flip side, I read Leyna Krow’s Sinkhole, and Other Inexplicable Voids very early in the year. The 16 stories take an askew, surreal, and frequently hilarious approach to the physical and emotional bonds that tie us together. The highlight is a set of linked stories about a family dealing with ecological collapse. Infused with a magical realist touch, the stories are about choosing connection and kindness over selfishness and hate.

![Multicolor cover of Colourfields by Paul Kincaid](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Kincaid-Colourfields.jpg)

Version 1.0.0

I end my 2025 Year in Review with Paul Kincaid’s Colourfields. His essay col­lection from the fledgling but marvellous Briardene Books is a reminder of how lucky we are to have someone of Kin­caid’s calibre writing criticism about our genre. Kincaid’s clear-eyed, precise, and astute approach illustrates a lifelong love for science fiction, fantasy (and a little hor­ror). This collection, which brings together essays about H.G. Wells, M. John Harrison, and Ursula K. Le Guin (to name a few), along with reviews of books about the field, is a terrific primer to Kincaid’s work. It’s worth buying for Kincaid’s emotional, funny, argumentative piece about his best mate John Clute written for the 2014 London Worldcon where Clute was guest of honour. If you care at all about genre criticism – and, frankly, if you read Locus, you clearly do – then you need to own this book.

That’s a wrap on 2025. Onward to 2026.
