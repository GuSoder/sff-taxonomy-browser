# The Year in Review 2025 by Gary K. Wolfe 

 February 1, 2026 January 28, 2026 Gary K Wolfe 0 Comments 

![Headshot of Gary k Wolfe](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/locus-1-248x300.png)

Gary K. Wolfe

SFF in 2025: Some Reasons Not to Just Go Back to Bed

I suspect that, like many others, I achieved my an­nual quota of dystopias and horror stories simply by making the unfortunate choice of being awake dur­ing much of 2025. Fortu­nately or not, little such fiction came my way in terms of reviewing – I did get a lot of publishers’ promo letters urging me to escape the bad news by reading several thousand romantasy novels, not to mention the proliferating varieties of “cozy” SF and fantasy (even “cozy horror,” whatever that might be). I’ve sometimes wondered if all this might be a distant echo of the passion for cozy mysteries in another troubled era in the shadow of fascism, the 1930s (which also had its own version of cozy horror, thanks to Universal Pictures). Maybe, like then, we just needed comfort; Annalee Newitz even managed to make a sort-of-cozy robot noodle-shop tale out of the aftermath of another US civil war in Automatic Noodle. But it’s important to remem­ber that the world in which we read the books of 2025 was not the same world in which they were written a year or two earlier, so that, for example, when Ray Nayler dedicated his Where the Axe Is Buried “For everyone who has lost a country,” he wasn’t necessarily talking to us.

![Cover of The Unkillable Frank Lightning by Josh Rountree](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/locus-7.jpg) Or was he? Though it was mostly set in Europe and a Russian Federation, Where the Axe Is Buried was one of the more chilling near-future SF novels of the year. Like the other compelling near-future novel I read, Ken Liu’s All That We See or Seem, it borrowed elements of the international thriller while exploring the possible influence of AI, and this leads to one of a couple of trends I thought I discerned, though neither were particularly new in 2025. The first is the now-familiar technique of genre-mixing. If Liu and Nayler mixed SF with the classic conspiracy thriller, Kemi Ashing-Giwa effectively placed a quest-fantasy plot into an SF terraform setting with The King Must Die. Tochi On­yebuchi’s Harmattan Season cheerfully blended elements of hardboiled mystery, alternate history, colonialist political novel, and supernatural fan­tasy, set in a vividly realized semihistorical Africa. Josh Rountree’s The Unkillable Frank Lightning continued his blending of SF/horror with the Western, this time using Frankenstein as a template. Pat Murphy’s The Adventures of Mary Darling combined bits of Peter Pan with bits of Sherlock Holmes, but also introduced some darker themes of colonialism and racism. Christopher Moore ventured into Tim Powers-style historical fantasy (and even symbolist painting and the early days of psychoanalysis) with Anima Rising. Fairy tales got reimagined as Gothic horror in both Nata­lia Theodoridou’s Sour Cherry (“Bluebeard”, and a disturbing take on toxic masculinity) and T. King­fisher’s Hemlock & Silver (Sleeping Beauty), while Nghi Vo’s Don’t Sleep with the Dead transported her version of F. Scott Fitzgerald’s Nick Carraway to a demon-haunted 1930s New York. In one of the bolder moves of her career, Nnedi Okorafor plopped an entire SF novel inside her otherwise realistic and autobiographical The Death of the Author, with its strong themes of family, disability, and literary life.

![Cover of Making History by K.J. Parker](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/locus-20.jpg) The other trend, even more evident, was the proliferation of dark academia, a subgenre (or just a theme?) which some mainstream critics have traced to Donna Tartt’s 1992 The Secret History, but which actually predates that by several centu­ries (Faust, anyone?). Two of the deservedly best-reviewed novels of the year were Emily Tesh’s The Incandescent and R.F. Kuang’s Katabasis, both of which feature memorable academic protagonists (and Kuang’s cosmology stretches all the way back to Dante); Charlie Jane Anders’s Lessons in Magic and Disaster also foregrounded academic research and the perils of graduate school in a novel that featured witchcraft, along with a more modestly scaled family drama. Something of the same might be said of Silvia Moreno-Garcia’s The Bewitching, set partly in 1908 Mexico but partly in a Massachusetts col­lege town in the 1930s and 1990s. (It’s inter­esting that the protagonists of the Kuang, Anders, and Moreno-Garcia’s novels are all graduate students, still in the process of defining their own lives. Perhaps that’s part of the appeal of academic settings.) Even K.J. Parker’s Making History, set among professors and scholars in his alternate late-medieval Europe, satirizes academic politics and rivalries, and in particular the uneasy relationship between the academy and the demands of the ruling class. That couldn’t really happen today, could it?

But enough of trying to make sense of things or find patterns. In addition to the titles already mentioned from Liu, Nayler, Newitz, and Ashing-Giwa, I actually reviewed a relatively sparse number of SF novels and novellas during the year. Back in the spring, it began to look like it might be the Year of the Simulation, with Daryl Gregory’s When We Were Real transforming the “we’re-all-just-a-simulation” hypothesis into a sharp, phantasmagorical road trip novel and Mary G. Thompson using it for the background of her promising first adult novel One Level Down. A few SF novels were marketed more or less on the edge of mainstream, and thus might not have been as visible as they could have been, though each has distinct virtues: Erika Swyler’s We Lived on the Horizon, set in a far-future AI controlled urban redoubt, explored some key issues of identity and technology, while Silvia Park’s Luminous, set in a future uni­fied Korea, did much the same thing; both novels injected fresh perspectives on the old theme of robots-becoming-persons. Laila Lalami’s The Dream Hotel is a chilling combination of tech­nofascism and prison novel; its depiction of the hazards of negotiating airport security began to seem unnervingly prescient.

On the fantasy side of the ledger, probably my favorite novella of the year was Amal El-Mohtar’s lyrical murder ballad of a tale The River Has Roots, though Nnedi Okorafor’s One Way Witch contin­ued to expand the saga of Najeeba, the mother of the protagonist of Who Fears Death, thus extend­ing the universe of that novel. Three fantasy novels stood out in particular. Guy Gavriel Kay’s Written on the Dark was as effective as any of his recent novels in his ongoing project of using fantasy to reimagine and recast historical periods; here the template was the 15th-century poet François Villon, though the novel also presents a moving recasting of the Joan of Arc story, and is written in a particularly haunting, elegiac tone. Joe Aber­crombie introduced a new series, set in his alternate medieval Europe, with the flamboyantly violent and hugely entertaining The Devils, complete with vampires, werewolves, an immortal knight, and various other monsters. But probably my fa­vorite novel of the year was Alix E. Harrow’s The Everlasting, a structurally ambitious novel with dueling second-person viewpoints, elements from Arthurian lore, parallel histories, time travel, and even a bit of the dark academia I mentioned earlier (one of the central characters is a hapless academic historian). Harrow’s portrayal of the highly skilled but deeply conflicted woman knight Sir Una in particular is one of the most vivid characters I met all year.

Of the collections I saw, Theo­dora Goss’s Letters from an Imaginary Country was par­ticularly outstanding, with its mix of literary explorations and personal narratives, while The Essential Patricia A. McKillip reminded us of the contributions to short fiction of one of our best fantasists. I also enjoyed Caitlín R. Kiernan’s Bright Dead Star, although the out­standing movie-oriented novella Zoetrope Bizarre wasn’t officially part of the collection (it was offered as an add-on by the publisher). Nancy Kress’s Not What I Intended, from the PM Press Outspoken Authors series, combined some excellent fiction with revealing personal essays and the usual lively interview. For non-fiction, PM Press also gathered Terry Bisson’s earlier interviews in that series in The Outspoken and the Incendiary: Interviews with Radical Speculative Fiction Writers. Another interview collection, Richard Wolinsky’s Space Ships! Ray Guns! Martian Octopods!: Interviews with Science Fiction Legends – more an oral history than a collection of distinct interviews – celebrated an older tradition of SF and the earlier, developmental days of the modern genre.

I’m not sure that I would characterize 2025 in general as any sort of a watershed year in SFF (there aren’t that many, anyway), but there was hardly a shortage of rewarding experiences like these, and watching the development of writers like Harrow, El-Mohtar, Onyebuchi, and Nayler, along with significant contributions from long-familiar names like Kay, Abercrombie, Kuang, Kingfisher, Gregory, Murphy, Parker, Moreno-Garcia, and Okorafor, and newer writers like Park, Ashing-Giwa, and Theodoridou. Of course, there were many im­portant books and writers I learned about mostly from fellow reviewers, and which I’ve added to my endless TBR pile, but then that’s one reason we read Locus reviews in the first place, isn’t it?
