# The Year in Review 2025 by Alex Brown 

 February 14, 2026 February 5, 2026 Alex Brown 0 Comments 

![Headshot of Alex Brown](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/locus-10-235x300.png)

Alex Brown (2021)

Year in Review

2025 was, to be blunt, a shit-ass year. The state of the world, the political cruelty, the worsening in­festation of generative AI, the continuing collapse of creative industries, the crumbling of our aca­demic institutions, all of it made me feel like I spent the year just trying to stay afloat. Fiction, in the form of books, movies, and television, provided some of the little joys that powered me through the worst of it. For some, fiction is a way to es­cape harsh reality, but for me it’s also a North Star. It gives me something to strive toward and hope for. Whether horror or romantasy, adult or young adult, doorstoppers or flash fiction, and everything in between and beyond, I loved engaging with stories about people facing an oppressive system they don’t know if they can defeat and going for it anyway. So instead of doing a list of the best books I read in 2025 or my favorite reads, I’m putting together a list of the speculative stories that felt like a light at the end of the tunnel, a torch in the dark­ness, a firecracker in the night sky, or a flash of cannon fire aimed at those who want to destroy me.

![purple cover of Coffeeshop in an Alternate Universe](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/locus-32.jpg) YA is going through a rough patch lately, what with publishers courting adult readers at the expense of teens and the oversaturation of the market with romantasies and romances, but that doesn’t mean there aren’t a bunch of great YA books worth reading. And if you know anything about me as a critic, it should be no surprise that my favorite YA reads this year were all queer. CB Lee’s Coffeeshop in an Alternate Universe is about two teens in parallel Los Angeleses, one with magic and one mundane, whose worlds literally collide. In A.R. Capetta’s Costumes for Time Travelers, teenage time travelers jump around the timeline trying to stop a bunch of time cops from destroying everything they hold dear. Rachel Hartman returns to the same me­dieval-ish fantasy world her other YA books are part of with Among Ghosts, about a boy who stands down fire-breathing dragons, vengeful ghosts, murderous bullies, and an abusive father while holding onto his compas­sion and kindness. Each book is different, but all feature a throughline of a queer community coming together to protect against external threats. With hor­ror, I’m recommending Logan-Ashley Kisner’s The Transition, about a trans teen trying to reverse a curse that is turning them into a werewolf, and Kalynn Bayron’s Make Me a Monster, a Frankenstein remix that questions power, oppression, and resistance.

![Black, white, and red cover of The Transition by Logan-Ashley Kisner](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Kisner-Transition.jpg) Vampires are in the middle of a renaissance lately in both adult and YA. If history is any in­dication, the rise of the bloodsucker is a sign that we’re headed toward an economic recession. Well, at least I’ll have these two wildly divergent takes on vamps to keep me company as my bank account dwindles into nothing. Dead & Breakfast by Kat Hillis and Rosiee Thor is a cozy murder mystery romance featuring two middle-aged vampires running a struggling B&B in a small nowhere town, a werewolf barista, and bigots who get their just deserts. Stephen Gra­ham Jones’ latest, The Buffalo Hunter Hunter, takes place in Montana over 150 years or so. This is the story of a Blackfeet man who is bitten by a “cat man” and turned into a blood-drinking monster, the increasingly unhinged Lutheran pastor recording the man’s tale in his journal, and a contemporary academic desperate to secure her scholarly future.

Some other notable, non-undead books that I think are must-reads: The Cosmic Color by T.T. Madden (gender dysphoria, militarism, labor exploita­tion, and kaiju), Harmattan Season by Tochi Onyebuchi (alternate history fantasy crossed with hard-boiled detective noir), The Death of Mountains by Jordan Kurella (Death and a heavily mined mountain in the Ap­palachian range have a tête-à-tête), and The River Has Roots by Amal El-Mohtar (a lyrical fairy tale about sisters taking on the patriarchy). The anthol­ogy Amplitudes: Stories of Queer and Trans Futurity, edited by Lee Mandelo, is revolution­ary simply by existing. In an era of rampant antitrans rhetoric and legislation, this collection punches back.

As you may know, I run the short fiction monthly spotlight over at Reactor Magazine, and because of that I have the honor of reading a metric ton of short speculative fiction each year. These are a few of the ones that even after all these months I still can’t stop thinking about: “What the Crab Apple Tree Near Miranda Space­port Saw” by Elijah J. Mears (Apex, June 2025); “Thirteen Swords That Made a Prince: Highlights from the Arms & Armory Collection” by Sharang Biswas (Strange Horizons, July 14, 2025); “The Night Market” by Erin Brown (Skull & Laurel, July 2025); “Gifted and Talented” by Marika Bailey (Small Wonders, Sep­tember 2025); “Highway 1, Past Hope” by Maria Haskins (The Deadlands, Spring 2025); “Moonrise” by Bethany C. Morrow (FIYAH, Spring 2025). As we slide into 2026, I hope you find the things that stoke a fire in your belly and rekindle your joy. Now stand up, dust yourself off, and get back to fighting.
