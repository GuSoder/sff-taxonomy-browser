# 30 More SFF Titles to Look Forward to in 2025 - Reactor

## 30 More SFF Titles to Look Forward to in 2025

Here are 30 more standouts to put on your TBR for the second half of this year!

By Christina Orlando

 Published on July 15, 2025 

![Collection of 30 covers of SFF books publishing in 2025](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Editors-Picks-2025-2-740x412.png)

I am extremely protective of my books.

Some of you may be like me—burned from having lent out a precious favorite and getting it back damaged, or never getting it back at all. Maybe you carefully write your name inside the front cover just in case, or emboss the title page, or have a little lending library card to record where your books go. I also know there are plenty of people who feel very carefree about their books and give them willingly, or don’t keep too many in their house to begin with. That’s… unhinged to me but whatever, you do you.

I was in seventh grade and I lent a copy of a certain magic school YA that was popular in the early 2000s to a friend, which I never got back. When I asked, she said she’d lent it to someone else. That, to young me, was impermissible. And so for years, I kept my books protectively to myself.

That meant I also kept my reading to myself. And it always felt incredibly lonely. This was—maybe not before people were talking about books on the internet entirely but certainly before there was a massive collection of bookish people all on one platform. So all I had was myself and my books, guarded fiercely by the hawk that is my anxiety.

Friends would ask to borrow, and I would point them to a bookstore or library instead. “I need it,” I’d say, even if I wasn’t actively reading it at the time, and more recently I liked to claim “it’s for my job”. I have broken up with people who asked to take my books. It just always felt like a little piece of me was missing when there was an empty spot on my shelf. I was always so aware of it, so worried about something that was out in the unpredictable world instead of safe with me. And then there is always the fear that something lent will not be as loved as you love it, and something between you two will become just a little damaged.

Ah, and then.

This summer I witnessed someone I care about read through a selection of my favorite books and oh, what joy.

I gave them Light From Uncommon Stars, The City in Glass, Silver in the Wood, books of my soul. We talked as they read The Raven Scholar (one of my favorites from earlier this year) and This is How You Lose the Time War (a certified banger), happily discussing lyric and plot and genre trends. To share these stories with someone else felt not only exciting but important—it is a way of connecting with people that lights up something essential in me. Books are a huge part of my life, and by sharing my favorites I was sharing a bit of who I am, of what makes me me. 

And what better feeling is there?

We tend to think of reading and writing as solo activities, but they’re not—otherwise we wouldn’t have book clubs or the massive popularity of BookTok or programs like Reading Rhythms or third space bookstore cafes. Storytelling is essential to community and connection.

For the past few years I have come to this space and written about books and the political—about how important reading is, how speculative fiction is a integral part of our culture, how fantasy and futurism help our hearts and minds expand, and more recently, how tough it is to keep believing in those things in the face of facism. Today I am thinking instead about the personal. I’m thinking about the delight of talking about books with my friends around the dinner table, or getting texts as they’re reading. I’m thinking about my mother, who is the source of my love of books, grabbing my hand after reading The Starless Sea by Erin Morgenstern, or asking to borrow my copy of the latest TJ Klune, or gushing to Gregory Maguire when we met him at Book Expo about how much she loves Wicked. I’m thinking about every book club of two I’ve formed over the years, little shared joys over having loved the same story. It’s that feeling of, “oh, you? Me too” that we all crave.

At the end of it all, this is why storytelling will always prevail. There will always be changes in format, there will always be evil people trying to erase important stories from our collective memory, there will, for a time, be fuckwads trying to tell you that reading a ChatGPT summary is just as good as reading the whole thing (insert endless screaming here). But our human souls crave narrative and togetherness. In this, we make sense of the universe and each other. In this, there is beauty.

That’s really what this list is about. I know that sounds cheesy in this context, with me on the other side of the screen. But I come here, twice a year, hoping you’ll find something to love on this list, because sharing stories is one of the best parts of living. Enjoy, dear readers. And if you do love something on this list, please DM me about it.

### Tenderly, I am Devoured by Lyndall Clipstone

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Tenderly-I-am-Devoured-by-Lyndall-Clipstone.jpg)

Sometimes all I really want is a vibey dark fantasy with equal helpings of romance and folk magic, something to really sweep me away. Fortunately, Lyndall Clipstone has come through with a gorgeous seaside tale of gods and salt and kisses. After having been expelled from school (for bisexual-ing too close to the sun), Lark returns home only to discover her family’s once thriving property has fallen into disrepair, and her family into debt. Their salt mine is no longer producing salt, and they owe everything to Alastair Felimath, Lark’s first love whom she’s since fallen out with. When the family’s (hot) god demands Lark’s hand in marriage in exchange for the salt returning, she agrees (which, like, same girl, I’d jump at that deal) to join him in the realm of the gods for half the year, a real Persephone-Hades situation. But the marriage ceremony is suddenly interrupted, leaving the fate of Lark’s family in the balance—and the only ones who might be able to help are (hot) Alastair and his (hot) older sister Camille. The emotional stakes are high, the vibes are impeccable, and everyone is sexy. What more could you ask for? (July 1, Henry Holt & Co. Books for Young Readers)

### The Bloodless Queen by Joshua Phillip Johnson

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/The-Bloodless-Queen.jpg)

You know, I’m really excited for a potential science-fantasy boom—there are several on this list, including this new one from Joshua Phillip Johnson. In The Bloodless Queen, the autumnal equinox brings death and transformation, as dead humans become fae and wreak havoc on the world before wandering into the Harbors, borderlands between our world and the fae realm. Fencers—government agents with unique magical abilities provided by numerical tattoos—guard against the fae’s destruction each year. (And get this, the whole thing is Reagan’s fault. In this alternate history, Reagan and other world leaders set aside half of the earth as nature preservation, which then turned into the Harbors, which then let the fae in. Hilarious.) Battling the threat requires both mathematicians and folklorists—which honestly rules, don’t lie and say you don’t sometimes wish there would be a supernatural emergency in which the billion times you’ve read Lord of the Rings would come in handy. We follow Cal and Evangeline, a married couple who work as fencers as they’re drawn into an international conspiracy, because of course the government is lying about stuff, and of course there is a megachurch of Fae Queen worshippers that is causing trouble. This is a high concept thrill ride with many layers to dig into. (July 8, DAW)

### Never the Roses by Jennifer K. Lambert

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Never-the-Roses.jpg)

Have you ever just been done with society and work, and wanted to fuck off into the woods? Go completely off the grid, build a big library, grow your own vegetables, make friends with animals? Yeah, me too. I get it. And apparently so does Jennifer K. Lambert, because that’s exactly what the witch Oneira is doing, after having retired from magical war-waging. And it’s sick, I’m extremely jealous of her life. She even achieves the ultimate dream of reading every book she owns. But obviously that presents a problem (y’all get it)—which leads her to infiltrating and stealing from the library of Stearanos Stormbreaker, her magical rival that she’s never actually met in person before. He is the only one whose power can match hers, and he also happens to be interesting, clever, and more than a match for her. (And we love a man with a big library.) This begins a back-and-forth between the two, a romance of both heart and intellect. It’s a slow burn, but an endearing journey as these two powerful people toy with each other and bond as equals. Never the Roses took me by surprise, and is an absolute delight of a book. (July 8, Bramble)

### The Library at Hellebore by Cassandra Khaw

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/The-Library-at-Hellebore2.jpg)

Cassandra Khaw puts the dark in Dark Academia with this one, I’ll tell you that much. There is viscera on the walls immediately. But that’s Khaw’s signature, and the beautiful thing about their work in horror—there’s no holding back. Alessa, after discovering all the ways she’s capable of magically murdering men, finds herself at Hellebore Technical Institute for the Ambitiously Gifted, a school for mages capable of great destruction. And when I say ‘finds herself’, I mean it wasn’t by choice. You see, the dangerous students of Hellebore are met with equally dangerous and mind-twisting circumstances—hallways painted with blood, creatures that look like men made out of meat, the creepy faculty, each other… AND they take your phone. Which is terrifying. Khaw writes women with teeth and claws and probably more teeth and a razor blade under their tongue, and Hellebore is set to be the crown jewel of their beautiful, bloody bibliography. And there are fucked up deer! I love a fucked up deer situation. (July 22, Tor Nightfire)

### The Memory Hunters by Mia Tsai

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/The-Memory-Hunters-.jpg)

I need you all to know that the notes I took during my read through of this one just say “this fuckin rules let’s gooooooo!” so that’s where I’m at with the latest from Mia Tsai. In The Memory Hunters, we follow Key, who comes from a long line of divers connected to the Institute of Human Memory. Key is not only incredibly good at her job (a process both religious and anthropological that involves mushrooms for all you mycelium sickos out there), thanks in no small part to her connection with her ancestors, but is also ambitious and enthusiastic about her work. Which is troublesome for Vale, her strong and capable guardian, there to protect Key during her dives and keep her from getting kidnapped by black market hunters. When Key stumbles upon a memory that she can’t shake, she knows it could be a major historical discovery—and it sets both Key and Vale (who have incredible chemistry ) on a dangerous adventure. The Memory Hunters is many things, but most importantly it is about intellectual curiosity and research, which is incredibly sexy to me. There is just so much rich history and worldbuilding in here—yet it’s a story that rejects categorization. Tsai is clearly willing and excited to break every genre rule with this one, and thank goodness for that. (July 29, Erewhon)

### Birth of a Dynasty by Chinaza Bado

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Birth-of-a-Dynasty.jpg)

Every once in a while a book comes along that makes me fall in love with epic fantasy all over again, and this season that book is Birth of a Dynasty, which from the start throws you into deep lore, complex politics, forbidden magic, myth, and heroism. This is a book that demands your attention, that will have you gleefully tracking family lineages and warring kingdoms through a complex tale of betrayal and revenge. M’kuru Mukundi, high noble of Madada, runs with his sister as their family home burns behind them, as their parents are slain by Prince Effiom. His sister falls too, dying to protect her brother, and M’kuru is the only one left. He flees, and lives as a farm boy until such time as he can have his vengeance on the those responsible. Elsewhere, Zikora Nnamani trains as a fighter, all too aware of the prophecy that claims the houses of Mukundi and Nnamani will come together to defeat the current dynasty. And y’all, it is just so rich—there are giants, seers, badass lady warriors, rebellion, sacrifice, all beautifully woven together. And of course this is the first of a trilogy, so there’s more goodness to come. (July 29, Harper Voyager)

### Mad Sisters of Esi by Tashan Mehta

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Mad-Sisters-of-Esi.jpg)

I have a crush??? On this book??? I have a crush on this book. Mad Sisters of Esi is the hot girl I heard about at a party (that’s actually true I did first hear about this at a party) and then fell head over heels for upon first meeting. The prose is so breathtakingly beautiful, and it’s no wonder at all that this one is the talk of the town. In it we meet two sisters, Myung and Laleh, who tend to a universe shaped like a giant whale. The whale of babel houses doors that lead to different worlds, which the two sisters are tasked to explore and record. When Myung starts to question their existence, it opens a chasm between them, and leads Myung to a whole new life unlike anything she’s ever known. With interspersed diary entries and academic texts, this book reads like an archive. It feels like Piranesi through the lens of a creation myth. Its sentences are devastating, its scope and intention are breathtaking. I just know you’ll fall head over heels for it too. (August 5, DAW)

### House of the Beast by Michelle Wong

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/House-of-the-Beast.jpg)

Alma has never known her father, and beyond a passive curiosity, has never needed him—until her mother gets sick, and there is no other way to save her. She’s desperate for help and agrees to leave with her birth father in exchange for her mother’s care, but he is a cold and fearsome man who, unfortunately for Alma, needs an heir. So Alma is taken to House Avera and into the Temple of the Beast, her arm amputated as initiation into the family (the cult vibes happen IMMEDIATELY). Of course this is a hellish experience for her, but during a runaway attempt she discovers a magical strength she never new she had. It is then that her childhood imaginary friend returns to her, only… he’s not so imaginary now. He is an elder god, the Beast her new family worships (and he’s pretty), come to promise her power and protection as she seeks revenge and forges her own path. But can she really trust the Beast? You know that feeling, when you start reading a book and the next time you look up it’s past midnight and you haven’t done anything but fall into the fantasy world? House of the Beast is one of those books. Equal parts high fantasy and romance, Michelle Wong’s skilled storytelling will completely sweep you away. (August 5, Harper Voyager)

### A Game in Yellow by Hailey Piper

![Cover of A Game in Yellow](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![Cover of A Game in Yellow](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/A-Game-in-Yellow.jpg)

Carmen’s girlfriend Blanca is being so sweet, trying new sexy roleplays and kinks and anything that might help get Carmen out of her head. Something just isn’t connecting, and their relationship isn’t what it used to be. But nothing seems to be working—that is, until, they find Smoke. Smoke is the keeper of a play called The King in Yellow, a play that makes its readers feel terror and madness and euphoria. Now obviously this sounds unlikely. I mean, I believe in the power of art, but how far can reading a play really go? However, Carmen begins to feel a rush like nothing she’s ever felt before, and also starts having nightmares and sleepwalking. With a screwdriver in her hand. Putting holes in the wall while she’s unconscious. That’s normal, right? Nothing to worry about. As they delve deeper into this mysterious play, a play that has a dark history, Carmen starts to lose herself. “Scared is the best way to be horny” says Eleanor Shellstrop, and apparently Hailey Piper agrees. A surrealist horror that moves at a clip, A Game in Yellow is thrilling and strange and incredibly kinky, and certainly not for anyone shy. (August 12, Saga)

### Katabasis by R.F. Kuang

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Katabasis.jpg)

We’re firmly in our Kuang era right now. And I don’t know about you, but I’m loving it. I’ve been hankering for Katabasis ever since there was a sneaky little mention of an academic romance by way of Dante’s Inferno on her Instagram (iirc), which is just a banger pitch. Katabasis follows Alice Law, magickal PhD student, as she prepares to descend to hell to bring back her professor—whose death may have been her fault, and whose recommendation she desperately needs. Unfortunately for her, so does Peter Murdoch—her rival, who things seem to come oh-so-easily to, whose carefree attitude drives Alice crazy. But they’re both top of their class, they’ve studied everything about going to Hell, and they’re determined to find their professor. What could possibly go wrong? Well, with Kuang at the helm, we’re surely in for an intricate and clever trip. Katabasis is a worthy follow-up to the massively successful Babel, so I’m gonna go ahead and call this Kuang’s Oxbridge set. (August 26, Harper Voyager)

### Beyond All Reasonable Doubt, Jesus is Alive! by Melissa Lozada-Oliva

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Beyond-All-Reasonable-Doubt-Jesus-is-Alive.jpg)

I need you all to get on the Melissa Lozada-Oliva train with me, okay? Not only is she a brilliant poet, but her surrealist novels (Dreaming of You, a novel-in-verse about bringing Selena Quintanilla back from the dead, and Candelaria, a multigenerational post-apocalyptic story complete with cannibalism) sing with wonderful weirdness. Beyond All Reasonable Doubt, Jesus is Alive! is Lozada-Oliva’s new story collection, and I will say it plays with genre in the most delightfully unexpected ways—a little fantasy, a little horror, a lot of whip-smart prose. Where there is discomfort there is also intimacy, and a keen exploration of trauma, identity, and womanhood at the fringes of society. Lozada-Oliva’s cleverness shines through a collection that will unsettle you in the best way. (September 2, Astra House)

### Wild Reverence by Rebecca Ross

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Wild-Reverence.jpg)

Vincent dreams of a goddess. The goddess in question, Matilda, is a herald to the Underling gods destined to travel the realms, and must learn to protect herself if she is to survive. She gains access to mortal dreams, and sees the boy Vincent dreaming of her—they’ve never met, but in his dreams they become friends, and a connection is forged. But the divide between gods and mortals is complicated, and fate has many plans. There are a lot of ups and downs here, but in the best way (a marriage of convenience! Deep lore! Divine politics! Destiny!), with absolutely delectable prose. In her adult debut, Rebecca Ross crafts a carefully plotted romantasy, each little detail a stepping stone in a sweeping emotional journey. (September 2, Saturday Books)

### Honeyeater by Kathleen Jennings

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Honeyeater.jpg)

Bellworth, a small suburban town, easily flooded and less easily left, is home to one Charlie Wren. People keep disappearing around Charlie—he’s not doing it, but it is worrisome and strange. He does keep meaning to leave town and set out on his own, but doesn’t for whatever reason. This time it’s because he’s got to clean out his dead aunt’s house. And after that he’ll leave for sure. But then something comes out of the creek… a girl caked in mud, leaves in her hair, and blue roses blossoming on her skin. No, not on— through her skin. The mysterious girl (Grace) shows up on Charlie’s doorstep, and so he is tasked to care for her. But discovering who and what she is might just uncover more questions than answers. This is a book that rewards patience and close reading. Jennings has such a command of language it’s astounding, her descriptions of the natural world and all its beauty are not only gorgeous to imagine but gorgeous to read on a sentence level. I ended up reading part of this aloud to myself like a weirdo because the words were just so lovely. Honeyeater is a dream of a book, a beautiful thing to indulge in. (September 2, Tordotcom)

### The Maiden and Her Monster by Maddie Martinez

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/The-Maiden-and-Her-Monster.jpg)

I am an extreme sucker for “there’s something fearsome and magical in the woods” as a premise, which is exactly where The Maiden and Her Monster begins: The Rayga is keeping Malka’s village in fear after several girls have gone missing, including Malka’s best friend. But so too are the soldiers of the church, who come to collect taxes and terrorize those who can’t pay. Unfortunately the church doesn’t believe the monster exists, so when a woman is killed, Malka’s healer mother is blamed. In order to save her, Malka enters the forest to find the monster. But when a close encounter lands her in the care of a golem—a tall (hot) woman-shaped golem—it sets Malka on an entirely different adventure that may be even more dangerous. Martinez’ debut is full of magic, myth, and a dash of “fuck antisemitism,” which absolutely rules. This is for the ones who know the woods are dark and ancient, and wander in anyway. (September 9, Tor Books)

### You Weren’t Meant to Be Human by Andrew Joseph White

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20300%20450'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

![](https://reactormag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/You-Werent-Meant-to-Be-Human.jpg)

The long awaited adult debut from Andrew Joseph White is set to be the horror release of the season, and for good reason. You Weren’t Meant to Be Human is the perfect blend of social horror, creepy crawlies, and situations so tense you don’t know how it could get any worse until it does. For Crane, the hive was supposed to be the answer. All it took was a sacrifice for the chance to start over as someone new, someone true. The hive offers deliverance from a life unwanted, and a safe community afterwards. Many of us would be tempted by an offer like that. Only problem is, then you’re part of a bug cult.
