# The Nameless Land: A satisfying conclusion with maybe (?) a promise of more

Posted by

Bill Capossere

´s rating: 4| Kate Elliott| SFF Reviews| 1 comment|

![The Nameless Land by Kate Elliott fantasy and science fiction book and audiobook and comics reviews](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/81fTIrgxmqL._SY550_.jpg "The Nameless Land by Kate Elliott fantasy and science fiction book and audiobook and comics reviews")

![The Nameless Land by Kate Elliott fantasy and science fiction book and audiobook and comics reviews](https://s3.amazonaws.com/fanlit/images/4stars.gif)

The Nameless Land by Kate Elliott

Kate Elliott’s The Witch Roads was one of my favorite reads this year, so I was greatly looking forward to its sequel The Nameless Land (2025), which concludes the duology. I didn’t quite enjoy it as much as its predecessor, mostly due to pacing issues, but it’s still an easy book and pairing to recommend. Inevitable spoilers for book one to follow.

In terms of background, the key elements of worldbuilding are:

- the existence of “the Pall” — the mysterious and deadly mist that decades ago settled over parts of the land almost as lakes or rivers
- the existence of spore: a fungus-like agent that seeks out any life and rapidly transforms it into monstrous shape
- a mythic tale of a “Sorcerers’ War” ages ago that nearly destroyed the world

In The Witch Roads, the action of the novel is precipitated by the arrival at the last outpost on the Empire’s edge of an envoy from the Nameless Land. Our main character, El, was conscripted into the High Prince’s party that is going to meet this envoy. An event greatly complicated (and here come several of those spoilers) by the following:

![Kate Elliott](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/71sbVboh4JL._SY600_.jpg "Kate Elliott")

- El, who has lived as a courier in the Empire for years, is actually an escapee from a horrible life in the Nameless Land, a feat she and her “sister” managed thanks to El’s mysterious ability to move through the Pall unaffected. An ability she has to keep secret, along with her true past.
- El’s sister died recently, and she is the guardian of her nephew, who comes with her when she joins the prince. By the laws of the land, he was technically “stolen” by El and her sister and so can rightfully be claimed back by his father in the Nameless Land.
- The prince early on is possessed by a “haunt,” a dragon-spirit known as one of the “Shorn”. The shorn ages ago sacrificed themselves to be sentinels against the return of the Sorcerers. The spirit fears he has sensed their return, and so “borrows” the Prince’s body to get to a place where he can explore further.
- The spirit and El grow attached to one another.

At the end of book one, the party has reached the last outpost, but not without difficulty as someone in the Court is trying to kill the Prince or at the very least prevent him from getting to the outpost. Also at the end, the spirit exits the Prince’s body to seek out if the sorcerers have in fact returned, much to El and his dismay as it means the end of their short-lived romance.

Whereas the first book spent most of its time in the Empire, save for some flashbacks, here our party quickly moves from the Empire into the titular “nameless land”, so called because it has been cut off from the Empire by the Pall. It turns out that some people in the Empire, including the Prince, were more aware of the country’s existence than others, and have incorporated it and its rulers into their palace machinations to become the next Emperor. Elliott, therefore, opens up a whole new setting and a whole new culture/society for us. In fact, more than one, as we meet the Nameless Land society and also the Sea Wolf society, or at least, one of their clans engaged in diplomacy with the Nameless Land rulers. We also see more of the non-human inhabitants of the world and learn more about them: the ones we see and the ones who have seemingly disappeared from the world.

There’s a lot of politicking and plotting going on, a lot of family in-fighting (not bickering but literal fighting) as nobles in both the Empire and the Nameless Land vie with one another to become sole ruler. Alliances are made and broken and remade, everyone is using one another so trust is tough, everything is transactional, and there are as well some surprising more personal betrayals, though I won’t go into detail on any of that so as to avoid spoilers. It can be hard to keep it all straight, especially when one gets into the details of hierarchy and relations, but it all makes for a nice bit of tension throughout as you really have no idea who going to do what to whom or for whom. We also get some more knowledge about some of the first book’s mysteries: a hint as to what the Pall might be, an explanation of the Sorcerer’s War, and more details on El’s past, all of which is appreciated, especially if this is the last book (more on that later).

It does take a while for the book to get going I thought. The first quarter or third or so feels like it moved in fits and starts, where the pacing is a little off, where we slow down for some on-the-nose exposition, but past that point the book flows smoothly, the last quarter or third are pretty non-stop action with high stakes, and the ending (or near-ending) is absolutely fantastic.

Characterization remains strong, as it was in the first book. I confess I missed the haunt, who is absent for much of the novel, as his wryness was one of the most enjoyable elements of The Witch Roads. But his absence is at least somewhat compensated by spending more time with a few of the side characters, particularly Xilsi and to some extent Simo. And the arc of the Prince from the time we first meet him to the end of The Nameless Land is wonderfully plotted out and extremely effective. Elliott does a great job of making him a rich, complicated character.

Thematically, the novel continues to explore the uses (especially the abuses) of power in this world, something we see in both lands despite some differences in governance and culture. One of the nice touches in dealing with these issues is how Elliott shows us that El is not above reproach (or self-reproach more accurately). For instance, as she muses on her belief that the Empire was a better place than the Nameless Land that “fed itself on the blood of the smallest and weakest,” she checks herself and asks, “Yet was the Empire any better . . . princes and high officials in the empire had the power to whip and to kill, and to use imperial law to justify their actions. Perhaps … she had trained herself to look to closely so as to avoid seeing what she didn’t want to see.” Another time she catches herself making a snap judgement based on the appearance of a non-human: “[she] was ashamed of herself. She knew better than to judge folk based on first impressions and old prejudices.”

The treatment of the downtrodden, of “the Other,” and of refugees is another theme that runs throughout the book. The Aivur, for instance, were all expelled by the Empress from the Empire as she did not “trust” them, and we see another clan huddled together in concealment with no place to go. Unfortunately both a timely and an ever-present topic in our history.

In fact, history — the way ignorance of it can cause trouble and also the way control of it can be abused to gain and maintain power — is yet another theme. As for example, the fact that while nearly everyone in the Empire thought there was nothing beyond the last outpost, a select few knew (and concealed) the truth and used it to further their own agenda.

All in all, The Nameless Land makes for an excellent follow-up to The Witch Roads and comes to a clear resolution of the major plot points, if not all of them, while answering most, but again not all, of the questions that have been raised. That said, the book also ends with a clear path to more adventures in this world and with these characters, and I for one would be disappointed if Elliott truly does leave both world and characters behind. So I happily recommend the duology with the promise that it does come to a satisfactory end in terms of plot, and a warning that it may not satisfy your desire to spend more time in this world.

Published in November 2025. When the royal party finds themselves in a land they never believed they could access, it will take all of Prince Gevulin’s (admittedly impressive) diplomatic skills to forge a coalition with an unlikely group of would-be allies. Meanwhile, as Elen mourns her lost love, an unwelcome visit to the land of her birth brings back the traumatic memories of the childhood she shared with her sister. And a surprise visit from an unwelcome family member threatens to derail the plans of multiple opposing factions. Loyalty cannot be demanded, only won.

[Spoilers, maybe]

If the series ends here, it’s on a note more typical of some of Philip K. Dick’s novels, where you get an ending emotionally appropriate for his viewpoint characters, but where the larger power struggles of the rulers remain unresolved. So we’re not sure if Thelan has definitely won out over Eleawona, and the fatal battle of wits between Astaylin and the crown prince has barely gotten started. And who knows what Luviara is up to. Or how matters with the aivur will sort out. So maybe we will get more books, but from the viewpoint of Kem? He, as a warden, is still involved with court factions, but what his exact status will be was not clear to me.

What I noticed was slowing down the tale the most was the excruciating details of travel from one place to another. Even getting from one part of Eleawona’s fortress to another took dozens of pages (and I realize some of that was needed to show the atoners’ passages and the conditions they lived under…but!). There’s a lot of travel throughout the duology, and it’s slow going with all the landscapes Gevulin’s party is traversing. If Elliott had been more concise on that score, the book could’ve been 300 pages (or fewer) instead of 400+.
