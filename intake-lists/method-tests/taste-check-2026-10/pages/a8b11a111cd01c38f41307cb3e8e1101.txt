# The Year in Review 2025 by Graham Sleight 

 February 26, 2026 February 19, 2026 Graham Sleight 0 Comments 

![Headshot of Graham Sleight](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/feb51a_opt.jpeg)

Graham Sleight (2022)

Year in Review, 2025 

It would of course be an understatement to claim that 2025 was an eventful year in world politics. But, given the lead times in writ­ing and publishing books, it’d be unrealistic to expect the specifics of that tumult to be reflected in the year’s fantastic fiction. That said, it’s not just been an unsettled year: It’s been an unsettled decade. Perhaps understandably, then, one theme it’s possible to extract from the year’s books is stability: that such a thing, if it were ever possible or desirable, isn’t available to anyone now. That may present in stories as questions about the stability of language, of setting, or reliability; or a sense that the status quo of their worlds needs to be examined as critically as possible. I don’t think it’s a coincidence that there were fewer books than I can recall in previous years dealing with the question of what the next 50 or 100 years might look like. (This is not a new thought: it dates back at least to Judith Berman’s 2001 essay “Science Fiction Without the Future”.) The idea that there’s a consensus future in SF does seem to be evaporating.

![orange cover of A Granite Silence by Nina Allan](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/agranitesilence_opt.jpeg) Another thing that’s been evaporating (again, not a new thought – this time due to Gary Wolfe) is the notion of stable genres. Nina Allan’s 2025 book, A Granite Silence (Riverrun), at first seems to be a true-crime narrative, but transforms into something much richer and stranger. Allan seems to me the most consistently interesting UK author of the fantastic at the moment. Robert Jackson Bennett’s A Drop of Corrup­tion (Del Rey) was a fine sequel to 2024’s The Tainted Cup, another murder-mystery caught in the cat’s cradle of a wider empire, with all the ambivalences that entails. Isaac Fellman’s Notes from a Regicide (Tor) was a marvellously rich reconstruction of family history, of politics and art, and of the unreachable, unstable past.

Nick Harkaway’s Sleeper Beach (Corsair) was a follow-up to Titanium Noir, continuing to ex­amine a world where a wealthy elite is physically superior to other humans. Harkaway’s irrepress­ible inventiveness and, increasingly, his political engagement were very much on display. Alix E. Harrow’s The Everlasting (Tor) was a sharply revisionist look at some fantasy standards: knights in peril and how stories get made or remade. Joe Hill’s King Sorrow (Morrow) was an almost nostalgic epic horror fantasy about the costs of making bargains with evil. But it was also an enormously readable epic horror fantasy, one that justified its 900-page length. Guy Gavriel Kay’s Written on the Dark (Ace) was a characteristi­cally fine and nuanced fantasy of history, one that highlighted the author’s concerns with those who make art.

![green cover of What We Can Know by Ian McEwan](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/whatwecanknow_opt.jpeg) R.F. Kuang’s Katabasis (Harper Voyager) was, like the author’s Babel, a venture into the dark recesses of academia. This time, though, the recesses led elsewhere, as the title sug­gests. Throughout, the reader experiences Kuang’s pronounced and welcome gift for narrative clarity. Ken Liu’s All That We See or Seem (Saga) was the first in an SF series and, at least, can be said to be a promising start. At its heart are hacker culture and the technology it engages with; Liu proves very adept at writing in a technothriller vein. Ian McEwan’s What We Can Know (Corsair) continues the author’s interest in climate change, with a setting a century or so from now in a world that’s endured multiple catastrophes. The pro­tagonist is not the only one to cast a harsh eye back at the early 21st century. Claire North’s Slow Gods (Orbit) was a large-scale and gratifyingly ambitious space opera, taking in a huge swathe of ideas: the sort of book that feels, in the best sense, fearless. Silvia Park’s Luminous (Simon & Schuster) was an exceptionally accomplished debut nov­el, taking old questions of robots, cyborgs and human-ness, and making something entirely new from them. Philip Pullman’s The Rose Field (Penguin Random House) brought to an end the author’s second trilogy about Lyra Silvertongue and her world of dæmons. It ended up as a mov­ing demonstration, in word and deed, of the worth of stories. Thomas Pynchon’s Shadow Ticket (Penguin) was, depending on your perspective, an alternate-world detective story; a chance to hear the unmistakable Pynchon voice once again; and/or a strong candi­date for the Great Cheese Novel. All three are very welcome. Geoff Ryman’s Animals (NewCon) was also a welcome return. Notwithstanding its closely observed Oxfordshire setting, it was perhaps the strangest book Ryman has written, and the most shocking. Joan Slonczewski’s Minds in Transit (Caezik) combined radical biological speculation with a richness of other ideas: a speculative novel in the best sense.

E.J. Swift’s When There Are Wolves Again (Quercus UK; Arcadia) is a powerful counter­example to my thoughts above about the lack of near-future SF. More than that, it’s clearly one of the best books of the year, examining how the UK might transform itself (and be transformed) in ways remote from the centres of power. Adrian Tchaikovsky’s Shroud (Tor) was a story of human-alien contact lifted from the routine by some very believable fallibility on the part of several characters. Emily Tesh’s The Incandes­cent (Tor) was a very different book from the Hugo Award-winning Some Desperate Glory. Its depiction of a magical school managed to be convincing both as magical and as a school – with all that term implies these days.

It was a good year for collections that offered retrospective overviews of authors’ work. Carol Emsh­willer’s Moon Songs (Third Man) was an excellent selec­tion from a very wide body of work: Some of the most distinctive stories of the last six decades can be found here. Theodora Goss’s career is, thankfully, very much still in progress, but Letters from an Imaginary Country (Tachyon) is a fine report back from her fascination with stories that build on older stories. I confess that I mainly thought of Patricia A. McKillip as pri­marily a novelist, but The Essential Patricia A. McKillip (Tachyon) provides plenty of counter-examples: She was extremely adept at short-form fantasy stories. Lastly, Lucius Shepard, Crows and Silences (Subterranean) collected four short novels by an author who was especially effective at that length. The Joseph Conrad-haunted “Ka­limantan” makes a particularly powerful impact.

Among non-fiction books, Cory Doctorow’s concept of Enshittification (MCD) is clearly an idea that arrived at exactly the right time. Paul Kincaid’s Colourfields (Briardene) is, as the subtitle tells us, writing about writing about sci­ence fiction. More than that, though, it’s a mosaic portrait of Kincaid’s distinctive and thoughtful (though occasionally grumpy) sense of the genre. And finally, Susana M. Morris’s Positive Obses­sion: The Life and Times of Octavia E. Butler (Amistad) offers valuable insights into a writer whose work seems more and more relevant.

Ten books of the year:

A Granite Silence, Nina Allan

A Drop of Corruption, Robert Jackson Bennett

The Everlasting, Alix E. Harrow

Katabasis, R.F. Kuang

Notes from a Regicide, Isaac Fellman

Colourfields, Paul Kincaid

Luminous, Silvia Park

Shadow Ticket, Thomas Pynchon

The Rose Field, Philip Pullman

When There Are Wolves Again, E.J. Swift
