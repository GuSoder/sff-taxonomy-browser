# Slow Gods: An ambitious novel that asks big questions

Posted by

Bill Capossere

´s rating: 4| Claire North| SFF Reviews| no comments|

![Slow Gods by Claire North fantasy and science fiction book and audiobook and comics reviews](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/91MK730UaTL._SY550_.jpg "Slow Gods by Claire North fantasy and science fiction book and audiobook and comics reviews")

![Slow Gods by Claire North fantasy and science fiction book and audiobook and comics reviews](https://s3.amazonaws.com/fanlit/images/4stars.gif)

Slow Gods by Claire North

Slow Gods (2025) is an ambitious novel that takes on a number of big questions about the extrapolation of end-game capitalism, the treatment of refugees, the responsibility of the collective, the effectiveness or not of individual action, the importance of ritual, and the meaning of life. If the ending left me a bit wanting, the vast majority of the book kept me engrossed thanks to a strong central character, wonderfully diverse worldbuilding, those aforementioned big questions, and North’s usual high level of craft.

The story is set against a backdrop of a galaxy home to a number of advanced and diverse civilizations, some of whom are part of a loose federation (somewhat similar to a UN) known as the Accord. Others include the extreme capitalistic, imperialistic, and belligerent Shine and the hive mind known as the Consensus. Maw, the main character, grew up on a Shine planet, where he is caught up in a riot, arrested and sentenced to a debtor’s collar (the Shine runs on debt and status). When an injury precludes the possibility of paying off his debt, the Shine makes him a Pilot — someone who creates a brain-computer interface to pilot ships through interstellar space by jumping in and out of the void between (“arcspace”). Most Pilots go insane after just a few jumps — the more benign civilizations limit the number of jumps, take only volunteers, or eschew arcspace altogether and use “slow ships”, but the Shine just considers Pilots disposable. On an early jump, Maw’s ship inexplicably is found in a wholly different section of space 12 seconds before it had departed, with all on board killed in horrible fashion and only a “copy” of Maw found alive. Just as odd, he can now pilot through arcspace with a preternatural precision and seemingly without any risk at all regardless of how many jumps he makes. Though if he becomes upset or “disregulated”, bad things apparently happen to those around him.

With that set up, the precipitating event for the story is the arrival of a god-like intelligence known as the Slow, who warns that a binary system is about to collapse in about a century, sending a life-annihilating shock wave through the surrounding 83 lightyears. The reactions to the warning vary. Some planets make long-term evacuation plans, the Accord at first is helpful but as the burden of refugees increases becomes less so, and the Shine basically calls it a hoax, says the danger is exaggerated, suppresses information amongst its planets, and doesn’t plan on doing anything to save any of the doomed planets under its domination, though they do stage an invasion of another planet so the Shine’s ruling class has a safe refuge. Because of his unique skills as a pilot and his seeming new-found immortality, Maw becomes involved with several of the planets over the many decades, sometimes involved in the evacuation (particularly with the historian Gebre on Adjumir, one of the doomed planets) and sometimes involved in the war.

Maw is one of those not-attuned-to-human-interaction characters (somewhat reminiscent of Murderbot) who often doesn’t understand what the “right” behavior is and whose outsider nature (he’s both described as other and self-described as a “monster”) can be used as a lens through which to examine human behavior. He’s a character who is interesting in his own right, whose viewpoint allows for intriguing questions, but who is also a bit distant and hard to connect with. I rooted for him but was rarely moved by him in the way I can be by Murderbot or the Creature in Frankenstein, say. But if not by Maw personally, I did find myself more emotionally invested in his relationships, particularly with Gebre and the sentient AI companions he is assigned (to avoid those pesky “disregulation” moments that rarely end well).

The worldbuilding, meanwhile, is absolutely fantastic. Several civilizations central to the plot are given a rich sense of depth and detail, such as the uber-capitalistic Shine, the Adjumiri who find meaning in every moment and through ritual, the sentient AI Quan (presented in a wonderful variety of forms), and to a somewhat lesser extent the hive mind Consensus (probably my favorite depiction of a hive mind I’ve read). Outside of these core groups, we meet a number of other species and though they’re given far less space, North delineates each with tiny sharp details.

Thematically, the novel is sadly both timely and timeless. A scene where a government gives an all-too-brief warning of a coming bombardment, blocks the paths of those trying to avoid the bombs, then kills everyone found taking shelter in underground tunnels (meaning they must of course be “rebels”) could easily be a news article with only the names changed. The lack of immediate response by some to the impending system collapse can’t help but call to mind our current lack of adequate response to climate change, just as it’s impossible not to hear echoes in the changing reaction to the refugee crisis, moving from compassion and an all-hands-on-deck view to compassion fatigue to “I feel dreadfully sorry for them, of course I do, but it’s all just … I mean, it’s just so much, isn’t it? Honestly, I find it hard to watch.” As for the Shine, yes, they are an extreme version of capitalism, though many of its elements can be pulled from history while the rest can simply be seen as a logical endpoint given the current trendlines. It’s not just capitalism that comes in for criticism though. The fecklessness of the UN-like Accord when it comes to doing something about the Shine’s slavery is sharply attacked, calling out the way they give lip service to rights and freedom but refuse to call the obvious slavery “slavery” because that would mean they’d have to do something about it.

One of the more movingly powerful themes is the impact of a diaspora movement from the point of view of the refugees, the way forced relocation means the inevitable loss of tradition and culture and leaves people feeling betwixt and between — not accepted as “real” members of their new home and having no true home to go back to. Here, for instance, is Gebre trying to explain to Maw why she is so committed to ensuring the evacuation not just of people but of artifacts as well.

There are already generations of Adjumiris being born who have never seen the surface of this world, never breathed its air. What will they understand of us? What will their children understand? We have a duty to try and explain, to keep some sort of connection. On hundreds of worlds light years away Adjumiri children are growing up who … may be told they are lesser. That their people did not make and build and dream like the natives of their new worlds. We must preserve something — whatever we can — to show them that this is not so.

And later Gebre asks of Maw, “What is the point of our lives if we are not remembered? What is the point of the stories we make if they do not tell people something true?” To which Maw had no answer.

My one quibble with the book, as noted, comes towards the end when we get a sort of answer to some of the questions raised (the more philosophical ones as opposed to questions of plot, all of which are resolved) and I was, as I said, underwhelmed or disappointed with what we get. But I don’t want to go into detail so as to avoid spoilers. It’s also the case that the book, thanks in part to the main character’s personality and in part to the somewhat fragmented/episodic structure (due to the long time range), creates a bit of distance at times between story and reader. Otherwise, Slow Gods is an excellent read with a compelling central character as well as several equally strong side characters, a complicated and engaging plot, a wonderfully rich and varied portrayal of different species and cultures, and a willingness to wrestle with some big questions. Highly recommended.

Published in November 2025. Slow Gods is the galaxy-spanning tale of one man’s impossible life charted against the fate of humanity amongst the stars—a powerfully imaginative space opera from multi-award-winning author Claire North, perfect for fans of A Memory Called Empire and The Vanished Birds. My name is Mawukana na-Vdnaze, and I am a very poor copy of myself. In telling my story, there are certain things I should perhaps lie about. I should make myself a hero. Pretend I was not used by strangers and gods, did not leave people behind. Here is one truth: out there in deep space, in the pilot’s chair, I died. And then, I was reborn. I became something not quite human, something that could speak to the infinite dark. And I vowed to become the scourge of the world that wronged me. This is the story of the supernova event that burned planets and felled civilizations. This is also the story of the many lives I’ve lived since I died for the first time. Are you listening?

BILL CAPOSSERE, who's been with us since June 2007, lives in Rochester NY, where he is an English adjunct by day and a writer by night. His essays and stories have appeared in Colorado Review, Rosebud, Alaska Quarterly, and other literary journals, along with a few anthologies, and been recognized in the "Notable Essays" section of Best American Essays. His children's work has appeared in several magazines, while his plays have been given stage readings at GEVA Theatre and Bristol Valley Playhouse. When he's not writing, reading, reviewing, or teaching, he can usually be found with his wife and son on the frisbee golf course or the ultimate frisbee field.
