# The Year in Review 2025 by A.C. Wise 

 February 24, 2026 February 17, 2026 A.C. Wise 0 Comments 

![Headshot of AC Wise](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/feb50a_opt.jpeg)

A.C. Wise (2016)

The Year in Review 2025 

It’s hard to believe we’re at the end of another year, or at the beginning of a new one by the time this is published. Whatever else is going on in the world, reading continues to be a source of joy, and there was so much incredible speculative fiction published in 2025. It was a particularly strong year for short fiction collections, so that’s where I’m going to start my look back, highlighting a few of my favorites.

Call and Response by Christopher Caldwell is a gorgeous collection inside and out. Neon Hemlock always does a wonderful job with its publications, but they went above and beyond with sprayed edges for this book, creating a lovely physical object as well as gathering beautiful stories. There are shared characters and set­tings, and many of the works speak to each other thematically. Overall, it’s a wonderful read. Tell Me Yours, I’ll Tell You Mine by Kristina Ten explores the horrors of growing up with a blend of literary fiction, cosmic horror, dark fantasy, and science fiction. Many of the stories are set in the uneasy space of childhood, featuring char­acters caught between worlds. One of the most chilling in the collection is the least speculative, “Mel for Melissa”, an excellent piece of body hor­ror that delves into trauma, guilt, and the brutal sacrifices athletes are expected to make for their chosen sport.

![Black cover of Teenage Girl Can Be Demons by Hailey Piper](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/teenagegirls_opt.jpeg) Teenage Girls Can Be Demons by Hailey Piper also deals with the horrors of growing up. It’s a strong collection all the way through, with one of the standout pieces being the original novelette “The Many Sins of Clara Greenstone”. Set in a home for orphaned girls, it drops a trickster character into a brutal setting where the residents must cope with abuse at the hands of the nuns running the home, along with the supernatural horrors lurking in the attic. The House of Illusionists by Vanessa Fogg offers up beautifully written and occasionally melancholy stories, many rooted in mythology and fairy tales, and others with a more science fictional bent. Altogether, the collection does a wonderful job of exploring loss, longing, and the desire for connection through the speculative lens, while also offering up notes of hope. Uncertain Sons by Thomas Ha showcases the author’s talent for evocative stories that drip with uneasiness and suggest additional horrors just beyond the margins of the page. The collection includes a new novelette, “Uncertain Sons”, which weaves cosmic horror through a story about family, ob­session, and revenge, and uses shifts in voice and perspective to effectively add depth to the tale.

Moving on to longer fiction, Neon Hemlock once again published some of my favorite novel­las this year. The Cosmic Color by T.T. Madden explores identity, dysphoria, and the guilt and trauma carried by soldiers through the lens of a story about a mech pilot who is beginning to lose his faith in his mission. The Iron Below Remembers by Sharang Biswas also features mechs, but as ancient artifacts being studied by the protagonist, whose boy­friend just happens to be the most famous superhero in the world. It’s snarky, sexy, and uses footnotes to great effect to present an alternate his­tory that feels rich, deep, and incredibly well thought out.

![Blue cover of Iron Below Remembers ](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/ironbelow_opt.jpeg) Psychopomp also put out several excellent novellas in 2025, so I was even more saddened by the news that the press will discontinue its physical novella line in 2026. I am, however, happy that they’ll continue publish­ing novellas and novelettes online, along with continuing to publish The Deadlands. No One to Hold the Distant Dead by K.L. Schroeder explores mourning and death on a planetwide scale in visceral and immersive story about a world on the brink of environmental collapse. Summer in the House of the Departed by Josh Rountree evokes the feel of classic Ray Bradbury, as a young boy spends the summer at his grand­mother’s house, surrounded by ghosts.

Published by Tordotcom, Psychopomp & Circumstance by Eden Royce is a lovely and quiet look at care and respect for the dead, set in a world soaked in magic. Don’t Sleep with the Dead by Nghi Vo is a sequel to the author’s novel The Chosen and the Beautiful, returning to the reimagined world of The Great Gatsby, where ghosts and demons and magic are real. The dead are returning, leaving the main character haunted both literally and figuratively. The River Has Roots by Amal El-Mohtar offers up a lovely take on murder ballads and fairy tales, while exploring the magic of language itself.

There were also several excellent novellas published in magazines this year, with two that really stood out to me. “Murder on the Eris Express” by Beth Goder (Analog) is a satisfying locked room mystery whose primary investigator is an AI who happens to be a fan of Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, but may have misunderstood Moriarty to be the hero of his stories. “The Chronolithographer’s Assistant” by Suzanne Palmer (Asimov’s) puts a unique spin on the time-travel genre as a young man with a fear of water takes on a job as an artist’s assistant to avoid going into the family fishing business.

It’s always a challenge narrowing down my favorite short fiction of the year, but I’ll give it a shot. “Because I Held His Name Like a Key” by Aimee Ogden (Strange Horizons) follows Alan Turing’s relationship with a fae lord, while ex­ploring the nature of the self and consciousness. “Never Eaten Vegetables” by H.H. Pak (Clarkes­world) is a heartbreaking story of a gestation ship trying to save 500 embryos that suddenly thaw and begin to develop far ahead of schedule. “We Begin Where Infinity Ends” by Somto Ihezue (Clarkesworld) is a lovely story of complicated friendships and environmental recovery, set in a postcollapse world.

“Shadow Jack” by CL Hellisen (Giganoto­saurus) is a surreal and evocative story about a group of boys forced to serve a religious order that regularly sacrifices them in hopes of ascending and becoming gods. “Faith” by Kate Maruyama sees a woman on Mars racing against the clock to understand her mother’s research, which is vital to the colony’s survival. At the same time, she’s trying to learn as much as she can about her family history before both per­sonal stories and research are lost to her mother’s Alzheimer’s. “Extreme Sports Club for Octogenarians” by Kate Lechler (The Deadlands) follows a group of senior citizens who take up dangerous activities in order to choose the moment and means of their deaths.

“St. Dymphna’s School for Borderland Girls” by Jocelyn Szczepaniak-Gillece (Weird Horror) is a sensual and uneasy story about a group of girls in a religious school who begin falling into ecstatic trances before mysteriously disappearing. “The Peculiarities of Hunger” by Woody Dismukes (khōréō) presents the past as a literal haunting, as a young, adopted boy from Brazil is visited by his dead birth mother. “Autogas Ferryman” by Champ Wongsataynont (Nightmare Magazine) sees a taxi driver trying to bring peace to the dead, but who is reluctant to face his own mother’s ghost.

All of that is merely scratching the surface of the wonderful short stories and novelettes pub­lished this year, but I did want to leave myself room to highlight some of my favorite novels as well. The Buffalo Hunter Hunter by Stephen Graham Jones is a powerful take on vampire mythology as a young woman reads diaries left behind by one of her ancestors, relating the con­fessions of a man referred to as Good Stab, detail­ing his long and seemingly impossible life. Blood as Bright as the Moon by Andrea Morstabilini is another unique take on vampire mythology as a small group of vampires attempts to journey to a kingdom on the moon that their fanatical leader claims is the true home of their kind.

Caitlin Starling published two truly excellent novels this year. The Starving Saints is a gor­geous novel that feels like an unhinged medieval religious painting come to life, as a walled city under siege is visited by four beings claiming to be saints. The Graceview Patient is a highly effective piece of body horror about a woman trapped in a hospital, undergoing an experimen­tal treatment for a rare condition, which leads to her losing her autonomy and her sense of reality.

Wearing the Lion by John Wiswell is a reimag­ining of the story of Heracles that pits the hero against his Auntie Hera, who is determined to see him destroyed as the latest embodiment of her husband Zeus’s infidelities. It’s a deep, rich novel that meets tragedy with hope, and confronts hor­ror with kindness. The Staircase in the Woods by Chuck Wendig is a wonderful exploration of grief, guilt, and loss. A group of friends returns to the haunted house that claimed one of their number years ago in an attempt to bring him back, while also dealing with their own demons.

The Bewitching by Silvia Moreno-Garcia layers together interconnected stories across three time periods, all involving mysterious disappearances and rumors of witches. The story builds slowly, but each section is meticulously created to come together into a satisfying whole. Space Trucker Jess by Matthew Kressel is an epic novel with a strong voice, following the titular Jess as she sets out in search of her no-good, con artist father, only to find herself caught up with galactic conspiracies and godlike beings from other dimensions.

As always, there are so many other works I could talk about, because it truly was a fabulous year for speculative fiction. Not to mention my towering TBR pile and the works I have yet to read. I hope, however, this inspires you to re­flect on what you read and loved this year, and helps you discover something new you missed. May the new year ahead bring you even more delightful reading!
