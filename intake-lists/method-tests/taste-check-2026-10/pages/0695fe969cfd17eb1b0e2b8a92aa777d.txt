# The Year in Review 2025 by Jake Casella Brookins 

 February 12, 2026 January 29, 2026 Jake Casella Brookins 0 Comments 

![Headshot of Jake Casella Brookins](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/locus-7-228x300.png)

Jake Casella Brookins (2025)

Year End 2025 

To begin my look back at the year with a cliché: It was a land of contrasts. Although isolating in many ways, I also found myself connecting with more fans and writers than usual – and meeting old online acquaintances in the flesh at conferences and conventions was a definite high point. In my reading and discussions in genre, the fragmentary or polyvocal nature of the field really jumped out at me; it felt harder than normal to draw any big circles around what was going on in speculative fiction, or to point to big tentpole novels that represented clear trends. At the same time, all year I felt various currents pull­ing at me in what I was reading – unexpected movements and connections around generic developments and resurgences, and the tools of the literatures of imagination being set to our shared concerns.

![Yellow cover of Harmattan Season by Tochi Onyebuchi](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/locus-22.jpg) Across all my reading in fantasy in 2025, I was struck by the depth and nuance of how imperialism is being thought through. Not a new observation, nor a new topic for speculative fiction – but the range of responses to empire was something I found useful as I watched the local & global news this year. Tochi Onyebuchi’s Harmattan Season was, unsurpris­ingly, really enjoyable and stylish; among other smart things it’s doing, Harmat­tan Season is clever and pointed in its examination of complicity in oppression, in how it lays out colonialism as an entangling and persistent structure. And yet it still has moments of brightness, of freedom. Although Onyebuchi’s use of noir is wonderful and easy to fall into, the novella’s occasional breaks from the form are exhilarating, and underscore a feeling of seductive patterns oc­casionally, decisively set aside.

I found two other fantasy novellas resonating on similar frequencies: K.J. Parker’s Making History and Moses Ose Utomi’s The Memory of the Ogisi. In keeping with a lot of his previ­ous work, Parker’s novella is darkly comic, in­tensely clever, and always doubting that clever­ness will be enough, in the end. It skates pretty close to nihilism, in what we can know about history or each other, but maintains a kind of lightness and sympathy throughout, even as it lays out the farces and atrocities power will commit to maintain itself. Utomi’s latest entry in his Forever Desert series is also about how lies and manipulation keep oppressive systems in place; while not as cynical as Parker’s on the surface, it has a well of sadness and thoughtful­ness about the cyclical nature of deception and the difficulty of lasting revolution.

So much SF dreams of taking down empire, and that, I would argue, is for the good. How­ever, the two fantasy novels that most grabbed my attention this year, Katherine Addison’s The Tomb of Dragons and Robert Jackson Bennett’s A Drop of Corruption, would also remind us that we live in a society, and that bad structures are best combatted not with vibes, but with alternate structures. Addison, returning to the world of The Goblin Emperor, has her sad and lovable detective-priest con­fronting a host of personal and societal chal­lenges, this time including analogs for the displacement and genocide of Indigenous peoples. If I once again found myself wish­ing that this world’s problems were con­fronted a bit more directly and substan­tively, I still found this a delightful read, particularly in how it links faith, empathy, and justice. A fantasy-detective adventure in a rather different tenor, A Drop of Cor­ruption, amid many biopunky terrors and wonders, is also, oddly enough, a dream of good government. It’s a romp and a riot, and beyond its locked-room mysteries and fascinating worldbuilding there’s a really strong thought – a screamingly timely one – about checks and balances, about shoring up norms and regulatory apparatuses.

![we lived on the horizon cover - desert window](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/swyler-we-lived-on-horizon.jpg) Switching gears while switching genres, here: In the science fiction I read in 2025, fun was an unexpected and very welcome recurring theme, as well as a return to and revitalization of classic forms. One of the most exciting and most clearly science fictional novels I read this year, Darkly Lem’s Transmentation | Tran­sience, is also very much about empire and the slow, sneaky, far-reaching ways that power accretes and corrupts. Perhaps more notably, it’s also off-the-wall inven­tive: a universe-hopping kaleidoscope of a book that is very knowingly shoving everything it can in the bag and shaking it all up, with a lot of imagery and ideas that call back to classic SF across the last half century and more. Another delightfully wild and un­predictable novel, Michel Nieva’s Dengue Boy (translated by Rahul Bery) felt extremely fresh to me in its exuberant voice and over-the-top satire, while still reaching all the way back to Lovecraft. Walled postapocalyptic cities and artificial personhood are hardly new tropes, but Erika Swyler makes them feel new in We Lived on the Horizon, which thinks through hierarchi­cal scarcity mindsets and the value of embodiment in a fascinating way. Fi­nally, a novel that is re­ally stunning in its scope and ambition: Pip Adam’s Audition holds contemporary issues very closely but is of a tradition with Philip K. Dick and Stanisław Lem – an astonishingly stylish and trippy spaceship novel, weaving together realities and evoking the best kind of estrangement and wonder.

![Red and black cover of Uncertain Sons and Other Stories by Thomas Ha](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Ha-Uncertain-Sons-and-Other-Stories.jpg) Genres were blending and blurring to various extents across much of what I read this year, and nowhere so notably or fruitfully as in the many excellent collections that crossed my desk. To pick just three: Leyna Krow’s Sinkhole, and Other Inescapable Voids is a great example – often built around surreal or magical-realist elements, the collection’s stories also include straightforward science fiction and climate fiction selections; what’s really interesting is how connections within the book create sympathetic vibrations and increased emotional intensity across those very different modes. Ed Park’s An Oral His­tory of Atlantis is a genre-bender that I am begging SF readers to pick up: in­credibly funny, charming, and constantly clever, with some really delightful love letters to odd corners of the genre. It’s also powerfully weird; some of its strongest stories aren’t easy to classify as science fiction or fantasy, but have a kind of potent, startling ambiguity that has had me pressing it on people since I encountered it. The weird is a unifying thread across many of the collections I read this year, and a weird one I absolutely have to highlight is Thomas Ha’s Uncertain Sons and Other Stories. Immensely stylish, this is an all-killer collection, mixing science fiction and horror to fantastic effect – another work that felt remarkably new but also has taproots deep in the genre, in this case reminding me of Bradbury and Ellison, among others.

My three favorite novels this year are all a bit hard to classify. Isaac Fellman’s Notes from a Regicide is definitely science fiction, though its speculative elements are intrigu­ingly subdued. Masterful on a sentence and structural level – this is a book so incisive that it frequently feels like an attack – it’s a deeply thoughtful work about identity, art, and time, and not to be missed. Another one that I’m worried genre folks will sleep on is Ethan Rutherford’s North Sun: Or, the Voyage of the Whaleship Esther, a gor­geous strange maritime novel about vio­lence, extraction, and their long echoes, with really haunting and horrific elements both supernatural and mundane. And, last but not least, a novel I’m pushing on everyone I meet: Eva Meijer’s Sea Now (translated by Anne Thompson Melo). This could have been a straightforward climate fiction novel. Instead, it defies ex­pectations, turns a simple story of rising oceans into a multi-genre mosaic that is funny, beautiful, and elegiac. Like much of the best speculative fiction, comforting or unsettling depending on where and when you’re reading – Sea Now is a reminder that change is the only constant.
