# The Year in Review 2025 by Maria Haskins 

 February 20, 2026 February 17, 2026 Maria Haskins 0 Comments 

![Headshot of Maria Haskins](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/feb48a_opt.jpeg)

Maria Haskins (2025)

Maria’s Year-End Roundup

I tried to come up with a neat and tidy list-structure for this year-end roundup, but the best I can do is a ram­bling, somewhat haphazard and certainly nonexhaustive journey through some of my favorite short stories, novel­ettes, and novellas from 2025.

Short stories

This year, it seemed to me that my taste in short fiction leaned even more heavily than before into the strange and beautiful, the strange and harrowing, the strange and fierce, the strange and… well, you get the picture.

I’m thinking of short stories like “The Heartbreak Hotel on Plutonic Planet” by Carolyn Zhao at Strange Horizons, where people who get their hearts broken end up instantly and mysteriously transported to Pluto, where they end up cleaning hotel rooms. The only way back to Earth is to get your heart broken again. Or stories like “Mud Soup” by Phoebe Barr in Bourbon Penn, where the magic of childhood rules supreme in the schoolyard as two friends turn old leaves, dust, and crushed-up insect wings into transformative potions.

Another story that crept under my skin is “It Grows Back” by Grant Collier at Cast of Wonders, one of the most unsettling horror stories I read in 2025. Like “Mud Soup”, this story takes place in the strange landscape of childhood, where the memory of a traumatic event can take root and grow inside a young mind, and where friends can get pulled into our darkest fears.

At Escape Pod, there’s Esther Alter’s remarkable “Magical Girl Antifa War Machine”, a brash and fierce story about a group of friends who are trans­formed into superheroes. It’s a story that doesn’t shy away from the dangers and complications (and advantages) of superpowers, and it’s also one of the strangest (and sexiest) stories I read last year.

Both the strange and the beautiful are read­ily apparent in “Woodpecker, Warbler, Mussel, Thrush” by Ruth Joffre at PodCastle, where a woman transforms into various animals when they become extinct: “It always happens like this: a species disappears once and for all, and I transform into a replica of it for one day.” I love this story’s mournful tone, and the way the nar­rator’s connection to the natural world is a source of wonder as well as grief.

One of my favorite flash stories in 2025 was “The Only Map is Memory” by B. Morris Allen in PodCastle, a haunting tale of powerful magic and two friends who change the world beyond recognition in a youthful game gone terribly awry. I love Allen’s haunting depiction of magic as a marvelous and perilous force that can alter the world around you so profoundly that it becomes impossible to find your way back to where, and who, you were.

“Wilayat in Seven Saints” by Tanvir Ahmed in Kaleidotrope, also stuck with me. It’s the story about what happens when the vizier’s historian comes looking for tales of saints and holy sites, only to receive stories of rebellion and resistance instead. I love Ahmed’s lush, beautiful prose, and the way he wraps stories within stories.

![Red cover of The Other Lives of Allagracia Sanchez by Felicia Martínez](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/altagraciasanchez_opt.jpeg) Novelettes

One of my favorite novelettes in 2025 was The Other Lives of Altagracia Sanchez by Felicia Martínez (Querencia), one of the strangest and most exquisite stories I read last year. Altagracia plays the piano, and as she plays, different lives come to her, sliding “in and out of memory, out of time.” It’s a story that is al­most impossible to describe. It moves and feels like a piece of music, with its own dream­like logic, like something that happens to you in the borderland between dream­ing and waking.

GigaNotoSaurus keeps publishing excellent sci­ence fiction and fantasy, and though they are on hia­tus right now, I am very much looking forward to the zine’s return in 2026. Two of my favorite novelettes at GigaNotoSaurus last year were “Horror Vacui” by A.V. Greene, where Hell is “tucked inside a dark offshoot of a cavernous walk-in storm drain”, and “A Good King” by Sarah McGill, a gentle, inventive tale that turns several fairytale tropes inside out and upside down.

Nkone Chaka’s “Riding the River from Below the Bowels of a Broken Home” in FIYAH is an­other novelette that has lingered in my mind ever since I first read it. It’s a story about a goddess/monster hiding in plain sight and working as a hairdresser. It’s also a story about the inertia and heaviness of grief, the impor­tance of art, and the truly transformative power of song and music.

Beneath Ceaseless Skies published several outstanding novelettes in 2025, including Peter Darbyshire’s “The Angel Azrael Visits the Trading Post at the End of the World a Final Time”, the latest instalment in his series of weird Western / supernatural / postapocalypse tales about the fallen angel Azrael. Another one of my favorites at Beneath Ceaseless Skies was “A Random Walk Through the Goblin Library” by Chris Willrich, a surreal novelette with an intricately twisted plot and a most satisfying pay­off. That plot involves a book that is also a lethal weapon, and a thief who must steal that book to gain his freedom. The trouble is that the Goblin Library is a difficult place to navigate: “You assume the library only occupies three dimensions. You hope you are correct.”

Two of my favorite novelettes in 2025 were published by Psychopomp. For reasons laid out by publisher Sean Markey in a forthright post online, Psychopomp will not be publishing any more novelettes (or novellas) going forward, and what a loss that is, considering the titles they published this year. Their April novelette was “UPDATE: The Buildings Are Hungry and the Plague Can Speak” by Natalia Theodoridou, which takes place in a future that feels only too familiar. Strange things are happening to birds, to people, and even to inanimate objects like buildings, and the landscape itself. These strange occurrences seem to be connected to a popular VR game that might be unraveling and transforming reality itself.

Psychopomp’s second novelette of 2025 was “Garden of the Bloodpotter” by Erin Brown, a beguiling and rich fantasy tale set in a kingdom marked by endless wars. A group of sorcerers called the Bloodpotters serve in these wars, and their magic is used to raise terrifying fighters called HighSoldiers. When a Bloodpotter turns up in the small village of Fauen, the inhabitants are terrified at first, but he seems intent on help­ing them, whether they like it or not. The Blood­potter also takes a young girl called Hen under his wing. Hen thrives under his tutelage, but she also knows the Bloodpotter is hiding something, something that is growing in his secret garden. Brown weaves a story of love and loyalty, betrayal and magic, that is epic in scope and complexity.

Novellas

I read several great novellas in 2025, including Casthen Gain by Essa Hansen, set in the same multiverse as her novels Nophek Gloss, Azura Ghost, and Ethera Grave. As far as I know, it’s the only science fiction novella I’ve read where the properties of different multiverses are used for cooking. Another favorite was the modern-day-gothic/paranormal novella The Cold House by A.G. Slatter, which takes the reader to some strange, dark, deep places and involves both ghosts and witches.

Three of my favorite novellas in 2025 were pub­lished by Psychopomp, including Josh Rountree’s Summer in the House of the Departed, a beauti­ful and heartbreakingly tender story about Brady, who spends his childhood summers in his grandmother’s house in a West Texas town. The house is old, inhabited by ghosts and filled with memories and magic. In 1981, Brady’s grandmother vanishes from the house with­out a trace, and everyone else in town vanishes, too. It’s a mystery that will haunt Brady for the rest of his life, and in 2025, he comes back to the house to try to figure out what happened. It’s a profoundly magical and melancholy story that holds so much love and so much sadness.

![cover of Summer in the House of the Departed](https://locusmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/summerinhouse_opt.jpeg) No One to Hold the Distant Dead by K.L. Schroeder, also from Psychopomp, is a uniquely imagined science fiction novella set in a far future where life on Earth is waning, and where people’s minds and memories can be beamed to distant planets to take up residence in manufactured bodies. There’s a lovely organic feel to this story that reminded me of Karin Tidbeck’s work, and I mean that as the highest praise.

The third was Starstruck by Aimee Ogden, a story set in a place where star-souls fall from the sky, turning all sorts of creatures and plants (and even rocks) into sentient beings. If that sounds strange, you’d be right, but you should also be warned that Ogden can, and will, break your heart with this off-kilter love story about a radish-person and fox-person.

I’m still heartbroken that there will be no more novellas and novelettes from Psychopomp, and I will miss Fantasy Magazine, which was also published under the Psychopomp umbrella, and which is now on indefinite hiatus. I am glad that The Deadlands will still be around, but it’s a sad thing to see a good publisher forced to pull back and do less.

Psychopomp is not the first ‘zine or publisher to deal with a dire financial situation, of course. This is an ongoing struggle for so many. So, what’s the moral of that story? That it’s tough times for short fiction publishers, just like it’s tough times for a lot of us. Support the ‘zines and publishers you love while they are still around. Subscribe if you can, support them on Patreon, spread the word about them online and offline. The world needs strange, beautiful, joyful, harrowing, disturbing fiction, and I hope to find much more of it in the year to come.
